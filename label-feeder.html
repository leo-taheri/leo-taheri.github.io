PROJECT 02 · MECHATRONICS & RAPID PROTOTYPING

Automated Label Feeder & Cutter

A compact laboratory machine developed to automatically feed, register and guillotine-cut groups of barcode labels — replacing repetitive manual cutting while preventing cumulative positioning error.

01 · PROJECT & PROBLEM

Automating a repetitive laboratory labeling workflow.

Laboratory samples were identified with barcode labels printed on rolls. The roll contained five labels across, while the same barcode could repeat over several consecutive rows. Each repeated group therefore had to remain together when separated from the roll.

Previously, users cut these groups manually with scissors. The goal was to automate the process: feed the correct number of label rows, stop in the gap between groups, cut cleanly, and repeat without damaging a printed label.

02 · SOLUTION

Sensor-registered feeding with a geared roller and motorized guillotine.

The prototype combined a driven feed roller, reduction gearing, adjustable web guidance, optical sensing, a motorized guillotine, custom electronics and embedded control.

Instead of relying only on a fixed motor travel distance, the system counted label transitions directly. This removed sensitivity to the first manual alignment and prevented small feed errors from accumulating over many cycles.

03 · ENGINEERING CHALLENGES

A simple-looking machine with tightly coupled mechanical, sensing and control problems.

01

Reliable label feeding

Printable rollers initially slipped against the label web. Multiple geometries and surface concepts were tested before a silicone-covered roller provided the required grip.

02

Torque & feed speed

A gear train reduced feed speed and increased available torque so the mechanism could move the large, heavy label roll reliably.

03

Web tracking

Repeated feeding caused the strip to migrate sideways. Adjustable guides and alignment features were introduced to keep the web centered and prevent tearing at the frame.

04

Guillotine cutting

Several cutting concepts were tested. A guillotine mechanism gave the best result, with blade angle, contact geometry and actuator motion iterated for clean, repeatable cuts.

05

Registration without drift

Fixed-step feeding could not guarantee accuracy because small errors accumulated. Sensor-based label counting was developed so each cut was registered to the printed pattern itself.

06

Sensor integration

Several sensors were evaluated. Ambient light and limited installation space required electronic modification of the selected sensor plus a custom light-shielding cover.

07

Plastic-first architecture

Most of the machine was designed for additive manufacturing, so stiffness, fastening, load paths and serviceability had to be engineered around printed polymer parts.

08

Less than three weeks

The working demonstrator had to be ready in under three weeks for a presentation, demanding rapid iteration across mechanics, electronics and firmware.

04 · MY ROLE

End-to-end prototype development.

I carried out the mechanical architecture, CAD development, printed-part iteration, feed-system optimization, sensor evaluation and modification, custom PCB design, wiring, motor integration and embedded programming.

The project was originally intended as a functional prototype before a more industrialized version. Because only one unit was required and the prototype already performed the laboratory task reliably, it remained in direct use.
